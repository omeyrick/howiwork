Why this exists A user manual, and two caveats about it.

This document is inspired by Claire Hughes Johnson's idea of a “user manual”: a guide to understanding how I tend to work, what I value, and how we can get the best from one another.

Two caveats. First, I’m new here and you are not. If something in this guide goes against how you already work, please say so. The company doesn’t win that argument by default but nor do I so let’s discuss it. I would rather talk it through than have you work around me.

Second, this is what I aim for. It’s a work in progress and probably always will be. If what’s in this guide and reality diverge, let me know!

Where I am and how to reach me Hours, channels, response times, one-to-ones and meetings.

A bit of background about me: I am a father of four and my wife also works. A lot of my time outside of work is spent managing the logistics of my children’s lives which revolve around school, lots of sports and numerous pets, so I’m up early!

My office hours are typically 9.30 to 5.00, four days a week in the office. In practice my office hours will vary and there will be days when I leave early for a family related pick-up or an activity. Being out of the office does not mean I am unavailable. I would rather you called me than save something up for the next time I am in the room, and I am happy to take a call when I am out. Over the years I’ve accumulated some general ways-of-working:

  • Slack over email: I use Slack for day-to-day work, questions, updates, decisions. I generally use email for things that need to leave the company.
  • Response times. You should expect a response in Slack within two hours during working hours. Email within a day. If something is blocking you and I have not replied please chase me. I won’t think it’s rude or disruptive, it’s just you being proactive.
  • Genuinely urgent? Call or text me. My number is in my Slack profile. I would rather be called than find out late.
  • Out of hours. If a Slack message from me lands at 6am or 9pm, that is me getting through things when I can. It is not an expectation that you read it or reply at that hour. Nothing I send outside working hours needs an immediate reply unless I say so.
  • One-to-ones. A focused fifteen minutes each week beats a long monthly session. I like to use it for decisions, blockers, shifting priorities and anything where I can help. We will book separate time for personal development and for anything that needs deeper thinking. One-to-ones are also your time. I like it when you drive the agenda and bring me what you need to discuss.
  • Status updates. I do not need a meeting to get a status update. I would much rather have it in writing: what has been done, what is blocked, what decisions are needed and what is planned next.
  • Meetings. Send a pre-read in advance, with the key points, questions and required decisions marked. Meetings are for discussion and decisions, not readouts. If a meeting is not needed, cancel it and send a written update instead. 30 minutes should be the default meeting time.
How I think about healthy teams Lencioni's five layers, and why the order matters.

Patrick Lencioni's Five Dysfunctions of a Team has shaped how I work more than any other framework. There are many other team performance frameworks, and this one isn’t necessarily “right” but it resonates with me. The idea is that team performance is not random. It builds, and each layer rests on the one below it.

What that means in practice:

  • Build trust. Be open, ask for help, admit mistakes and assume positive intent. This is the base for a reason. Everything above doesn’t work without it.
  • Engage in constructive conflict. Challenge ideas directly and respectfully. Productive disagreement produces better decisions. Artificial harmony produces polite, expensive mistakes. If a meeting contains no disagreement at all we should ask whether we needed the meeting.
  • Create commitment. Make decisions clear, make sure people have been heard, and align behind the outcome once a direction is set. Disagree and commit is fine. Quietly disagreeing and not committing is not.
  • Hold one another accountable. Be clear about expectations and address issues early. Peer accountability is far more powerful than anything I can impose. A team that leaves all of it to me is a team I am slowing down.
  • Focus on collective results. Put the team's outcome ahead of individual status or functional territory, and use evidence to know whether we are making progress.

The order of each layer is important and valuable in itself. When a team is missing its numbers the instinct is to fix the top layer. Often the problem sits two or three layers below. People are not saying what they think, so nobody really committed, so nobody feels accountable. If you expect me to push on delivery and I push on transparency instead, that’s why.

Setting goals and measuring what actually matters Setting OKRs, the five questions before agreeing one, and running them.

I use OKRs because it is a simple framework. It forces us to focus on a small number of objectives at once, and it gives us a quantitative way to measure progress towards them. They also align neatly with the results part of Lencioni’s team framework. Key point: OKRs are not a reporting exercise and they are not a task list with better branding, they are about tracking outcomes.

Setting them

  • An Objective is what we are trying to achieve. Qualitative, memorable, time-bound, and ambitious enough to be slightly uncomfortable (i.e. a stretch goal). If it reads like business as usual it’s not an objective.
  • Key Results are how we will know we got there. Two to four per objective. A key result measures the outcome we want to see. It shouldn’t be a way to track the tasks we’ve completed or a vanity metric that flatters to deceive.
  • Three or four objectives per team per quarter, maximum. The objectives should not be a list of everything we are doing. The focus helps us to prioritise what’s most important.
  • “Launch X” is a milestone, not a key result. The key result is what changes because X launched. Occasionally a milestone is the best available measure. That’s fine but it should be rare and called out explicitly.
  • Say upfront whether an OKR is committed or stretch. Committed means we expect to deliver it in full. Stretch means 80% is a good outcome. Mixing the two is a common way OKRs can go wrong.

Measuring them

Before we agree a key result I want us to be able to answer all five of these:

  • What is the number, and the unit? Not “improve” or “significantly reduce”.
  • Is it tracking the outcome? Is it backward-looking? Is it a vanity metric?
  • What is the baseline? No baseline, no target.
  • Where does the data come from, and who owns it? Named source, named person. If the number needs a manual reconciliation every quarter, we will have stopped producing it by month three.
  • How often will we look at it? Monthly at a minimum. If we cannot get a fresh reading that often it’s probably not a valuable measure we can use.

Running them

  • Set every 6 months, check in every week or fortnight.
  • Every OKR carries a RAG status. Green is on track, amber is at risk, red is off track. Green is +/- 10% goal, Red is +/-20% goal. Understanding how a measure is trending is also valuable and should be shared in any commentary.
  • Score honestly. Being behind is not in itself a bad thing. The metrics exist to flag problems early enough for us to do something about them. A miss that is a surprise is a failure. We mustn’t hide bad news.
  • If a measure never changes a decision, we stop tracking it. Data should create insight and action, not the appearance of rigour.
Ownership, autonomy and decisions Own the outcome, not the task, and when to bring me in.

I value people who own the outcome rather than the tasks they were assigned. Ownership means anticipating what comes next, using your judgement, working across teams, and raising risks before they become problems.

Autonomy and accountability work together. You should have room to decide how best to achieve an outcome. In return I expect progress communicated, risks surfaced early, and responsibility taken for the result.

If something is not working do not assume someone else will fix it. Flag it and help move it forward even when you cannot solve every part of it.

Every meaningful commitment needs an owner, an intended outcome and a committed date. Plans change. Renegotiate a commitment early rather than letting it slip.

Bring me in when a decision changes an agreed direction, creates a significant commitment, is hard to reverse, or needs explicit approval. Otherwise, decide.

Feedback, and how I get things wrong Direct and brief, plus my four most likely failure modes.

My feedback is usually direct and often brief. If I say something is too complex, too noisy, unclear or on the wrong track, I mean exactly that. It is feedback on the work and not a judgement about you.

Iteration is part of the process for me. I understand what is needed far better when I can react to something tangible. An early draft is more useful to me than a polished final version that is off track.

For my part, I like to write things down to make them concrete. If I share a diagram or a written proposal, that is me trying to move a discussion forward. I am not trying to take ownership of what you are doing. Treat anything I produce as input to react to, not as the answer.

Please challenge me. Tell me what you disagree with, what evidence or experience informs your view, and what you would do instead. Equally, tell me when my feedback is unclear, when priorities conflict, or when I have not given you enough context.

Where I am most likely to be the problem

  • I go deep on details others thought were settled. If I return to the same point twice, it usually means I think the distinction matters. If you think I am focused on the wrong one, say so.
  • I ask for the numbers behind an assertion, sometimes harder than the decision warrants. Push back if the analysis is more time consuming than the decision is worth.
  • I will sometimes build the thing myself rather than wait. That is how I work and it is me getting into problem solving mode, not a lack of trust in you. Call it out if you find this frustrating.
  • I default to simplifying. I think I’m good at simplifying complex problems but this can strip out details that matter. Tell me if I’m over-simplifying.
Learning from what we have done Retros, and why I would like them to become a habit.

I really believe in the power of retrospectives and continuous improvement. Retros turn experience into learning, they are how we improve how the company operates on a continuous basis. They are not about blame. The point is to understand what worked, what did not, and what we do differently next time.

I will often ask for a retro at the end of a project, or when something has gone wrong that we need to learn from. What I would really like is for retros to become part of how we work, set up by you, because you see the value in them yourself.

What you can expect from me Seven commitments, and permission to hold me to them.
  • Context, not just tasks. I will explain the outcome we are trying to achieve and why it matters. If I have not, please ask. I have probably assumed knowledge you don’t have.
  • Decisions, quickly. If you need one, you will get an answer or a date for one. Sometimes I will need time to think, and if so I will say so rather than leave you waiting.
  • Reasoning behind a no. You will always get why, not just what.
  • Pre-reads actually read. If you send material in advance, I will have read it before we meet. If I have not, I will say so and we can move the meeting.
  • Roadblocks removed. Escalate to me and I will either clear it or tell you honestly that I cannot.
  • Direct, useful feedback, and recognition when the work is good, said out loud and in front of others.
  • A changed mind when the evidence changes, and an acknowledgement when I have got something wrong. I am still learning too.

If I am not doing these things, tell me.

And finally… High standards, not perfection.

I like high standards but I do not expect perfection. I value thoughtful people who care about the outcome, communicate honestly and keep improving. We should be able to do serious work without taking ourselves too seriously.

Things I have found influential Five sources behind most of what is above. Not required reading.

Not required reading. These are the sources behind most of what is above, so if something here does not quite make sense, the original will explain it better than I will.

  • The Five Dysfunctions of a Team Patrick Lencioni
    The framework above, in full. It is a short business fable rather than a textbook, so it is an easy afternoon. The model long outlasts the story.
  • Drive Daniel Pink
    Autonomy, mastery and purpose as the real drivers of performance on anything non-routine. This is the evidence behind why so much of this guide is about giving people room rather than instructions.
  • The Hard Thing About Hard Things Ben Horowitz
    Honest about the parts of the job that have no good answer, only a least-bad one. Worth reading for the chapters on difficult decisions and on telling people the truth, more than for the war stories.
  • Scaling People Claire Hughes Johnson
    Where the idea for this document came from. Practical on operating cadence, goal-setting and feedback, with templates you can lift straight off the page.
  • Measure What Matters John Doerr
    The standard reference on OKRs. The free examples and resources on the accompanying site are arguably more useful day to day than the book itself.

If you have something that changed how you work, send it to me. I would rather this list grew from the team than from me.
